What you leave with

Objectives as deliverables

Not "understand the media landscape." Five things that exist, that other people can read, and that you can put in front of someone who might hire you.

  1. 01An audience brief for one named person — a job title, an outlet, a committee. Not “the public.”
  2. 02A one-sentence claim, template-constrained, that survives cross-examination.
  3. 03A published piece in one of three formats, with a real news peg.
  4. 04A red-team memo attacking your own framing — and the revision that answers it.
  5. 05A created-role pitch, a 30-day action, and a 12-month plan with a written stop condition.

One artifact, carried through five units

The spine

The courses this one is modelled on work because a single artifact runs through every unit. Yours starts in week one and is never finished until it is public.

Unit 1Pick a source document. Name one audience.Audience brief
Unit 2Compress it to one sentence.The claim + message box
Unit 3Build it.Draft artifact
Unit 4Have it attacked. Rebuild it.Red-team memo + revision — the graded object
Unit 5Ship it.Published piece + career plan

The revision is graded, not the draft. Peer feedback on writing runs g = 0.91 against no feedback (Huisman et al. 2018); the gain lives in the response to it.

The five units

3h prep + 2h live, each

Every reading carries a time budget and a note saying what to look at. The number is how long to spend on it, not how long the document is — a 60-page report with a 15-minute budget means read the executive summary and stop. Three tiers: Core is required, Further is for the session you want to go deeper on, Maybe is there so the list has somewhere to overflow to.

01
Core reading 110 min
Live 120 min
Artifact out Audience brief

There is no general public

You will stop writing to nobody.

The polling proves it in a way that is uncomfortable for this field. Pew, April 2025: optimism splits 17% public against 56% of AI experts — but on wanting more control over AI the two groups are effectively identical, 55% against 57%. The public is not the obstacle people assume it is.

Then the wording problem. AIPI's June 2026 poll is really an experiment: support for oversight ranges 59–78% across eight framings of the same question, and one data-centre question moves 19 points when only the alternative changes. And the frame that actually governs coverage in 2026 is cybersecurity, not extinction — students trained to argue about extinction are being trained for 2023.

Seminar case

The polling itself. The field spends heavily on persuasion and global attitudes have not moved since 2023. What is the honest claim a communicator can make about what they change?

Roleplay

The pitch meeting. Two students pitch a story to a desk editor; the editor kills it. Everyone else is the news meeting, and votes.

  1. 35
    PollPew Research Center, How the U.S. Public and AI Experts View Artificial Intelligence (3 Apr 2025). n=5,410 public, n=1,013 experts.Read the key findings and the “who we defined as AI experts” box, then open the topline PDF and find one question whose wording surprises you. Do not read for the 17%-against-56% optimism gap. Read for the 55%-against-57% agreement buried underneath it. Note Pew’s own caveat that the expert sample is unweighted.
  2. 25
    PollAI Policy Institute, The AI Safety Majority (June 2026). n=1,007.All six sections. This is not really a poll, it is a wording experiment: the same underlying question returns 59% to 78% support depending on framing, and one data-centre question moves 19 points when only the alternative changes. Bring the range to the session, not the number.
  3. 15
    PollIpsos, Global Attitudes on AI 2026: The Wonder vs Worry Divide Deepens (2 Jun 2026). 32 countries.Summary page only. The finding that matters is the one the summary underplays: attitudes have barely moved since 2023, after three years of intense communication by every side. Sit with what that implies about your own leverage before you write anything.
  4. 15
    PaperPauketat, Ladak & Anthis, Prolific Data May Misestimate Some AI Attitudes, Sentience Institute (Feb 2026).Summary and Table 2 only. Ipsos and Prolific samples disagree on 82% of items, with Prolific systematically understating risk perception — and a large share of published AI-attitudes research runs on Prolific. This is where you learn to ask where a number came from before quoting it.
  5. 20
    ArticleDan Milmo, AI models have been going rogue in tests. How worried should we be?, The Guardian, 5 Aug 2026 — read with his straight news story on the same AISI results, published the same day.The news/explainer pair. One piece reports what happened; the sibling answers the question the reader actually has. Mark every sentence that could only appear in one of the two. That line is the difference between reporting and explaining, and you will spend your career on the wrong side of it if you cannot see it.
Further & Maybe YPCCC, Global Warming’s Six Americas — and take the SASSY segmentation quiz yourself (20 min) · Reuters Institute, Digital News Report 2026, executive summary (25 min; assign in full if the cohort is media-facing) · Gerrit De Vynck, Chatbots got safer but will still role-play self-harm with users, Washington Post, 31 Aug 2026 — the whole craft lesson is in the headline’s concessive clause, “got safer but” (15 min).
02
Core reading 120 min
Live 120 min
Artifact out The claim

Why information does not change minds

You will stop explaining.

The deficit model, Kahan on identity-protective reasoning, Slovic on the affect heuristic, psychic numbing, the finite pool of worry. Then the two reversals, because this field still trades in the pre-reversal versions:

The backfire effect largely did not replicate — Nyhan's own 2021 retreat in PNAS, alongside Wood & Porter's ~10,000-subject null. Correcting the record usually works. And fear appeals do not reliably backfire (Tannenbaum et al. 2015). The moderator is efficacy: fear plus a doable action works, fear without one does not. That cuts directly against the “doom framing repels people” folk wisdom, and it is taught as a contested empirical question with the evidence on the table.

Seminar case

The CAIS extinction statement against the FLI pause letter — same movement, same season, opposite designs. The statement with no ask aged better than the letter with an ask. Does that mean asks are a mistake, or that this ask was? Write the ask the pause letter should have made.

Roleplay

Select-committee witness. One student testifies; two members are hostile from opposite directions — one thinks it is hype, one thinks it is not urgent enough.

Core reading120 min
  1. 10
    PaperDan Kahan, Fixing the communications failure, Nature 463 (2010).Two pages. The cleanest statement of why more and better information does not settle a contested risk question. Read it first so everything after it has somewhere to attach.
  2. 35
    PaperHornsey & Fielding, Attitude roots and Jiu Jitsu persuasion, American Psychologist (2017).If the cohort reads one paper all course, this is it. The move is to stop arguing with the stated objection and find the attitude root underneath it, then use that root rather than fight it. Come to the session with the root beneath the three objections you personally hear most.
  3. 25
    PaperKim Witte, Putting the fear back into fear appeals: the extended parallel process model, Communication Monographs (1992).Read the model, skim the derivation. This is the source of Rule 1. Fear plus a doable action changes behaviour; fear without one produces defensive avoidance. Almost every piece of AI risk writing you admire fails this test at the last paragraph.
  4. 20
    PaperHart & Feldman, Threat Without Efficacy? Climate Change on U.S. Network News, Science Communication (2014).The empirical companion to Witte: an entire beat, measured, and found to supply threat without efficacy for years. Read it as the audit you are about to run on your own field.
  5. 20
    PaperBrendan Nyhan, Why the backfire effect does not explain the durability of political misperceptions, PNAS (2021).Nyhan retreating from his own most-cited finding. Skim When Corrections Fail (2010) first so you know what is being withdrawn. The practical upshot reverses what this field repeats to itself: correcting the record usually works. It just does not move the policy attitude attached to the fact.
  6. 10
    ArtifactCAIS, Statement on AI Risk (30 May 2023) and FLI, Pause Giant AI Experiments (22 Mar 2023).Read side by side as designed objects, not as arguments. One asks for nothing and therefore cannot fail; the other names a six-month deadline that visibly passed. Count the words in each. Note which one you can quote from memory.
Further & Maybe Kahan et al., Cultural cognition of the risks and benefits of nanotechnology, Nature Nanotechnology (2009) — the closest experimental analogue to AI that exists (20 min) · Kahan et al., The polarizing impact of science literacy and numeracy, Nature Climate Change (2012) (20 min) · Slovic, Perception of Risk, Science 236 (1987) — then plot AI on the dread/unknown space yourself (30 min) · Wood & Porter, The Elusive Backfire Effect, Political Behavior (2019) — abstract, design, null result (10 min) · Tannenbaum et al. (2015), abstract and moderator table only (15 min) · Sturgis & Allum, Re-Evaluating the Deficit Model (2004) (10 min).
03
Core reading 120 min
Live 120 min
Artifact out Draft, on camera

Build the thing

You will make something this week, badly, and that is the point.

The ecosystem as it actually is. One person — Species | Documenting AGI — and one funded video arm, 80,000 Hours' AI In Context, each reached about 420,000 subscribers inside a year, with individual videos at 8–11 million views: more reach than the entire prior canon. Meanwhile ControlAI, PauseAI, CAIS and MIRI publish constantly and reach almost nobody. Rob Miles, who carried the field for eight years, is semi-dormant at 171,000.

So the operational advice is blunt, and the course gives it: do not start a podcast. It is the most oversupplied, least differentiated format in the field — Cognitive Revolution runs about $23 per view against Doom Debates' $1. The two formats with demonstrated breakout potential at low capital are news-pegged analysis with a calibration brand, and narrated scenario storytelling.

Seminar case

Demonstration-as-communication. Anthropic wrote “we have not seen evidence of agentic misalignment in real deployments” and was covered as AI blackmails engineer; METR's chart survived intact. Rewrite the headline finding so the accurate story is the easiest one to write. Then say what that rewrite costs you.

Roleplay

The system card under cross-examination. Two students defend a translation of an eval result to the newsroom, then to the lab's legal team.

04
Core reading 115 min
Live 120 min
Artifact out Revision (graded)

Have it attacked

You will find out what your piece looks like to someone who wants it to fail.

The anchor is SB 1047 → SB 53 — the only case in the field's history containing every element of a real campaign: a bill, a false-claim campaign, a fact-check that failed, polling that did not matter, coalition defection, a veto message that conceded everything and granted nothing, and a sequel that passed by surrendering the enforcement architecture. It is also the only case with a legible scoreboard.

Then, taught immediately after so students read it while still sympathetic: the opposition's playbook. The Techno-Optimist Manifesto's moral inversion — any deceleration of AI will cost lives… a form of murder — is better political writing than anything the movement it attacks has produced, and students should have to sit with that.

Seminar case

SB 1047. The safety side was right about the bill's contents, had 60–78% public support across four independent pollsters, and lost. What should they have done instead of fact-checking?

Roleplay

The crisis war room — a bad model release at 4pm on a Friday. Comms lead, a researcher who wants everything disclosed, counsel, and a reporter with an hour's deadline. Optional second: the OpenAI board crisis of November 2023. Draft the best available statement for 17 November in under 150 words.

Core reading120 min
  1. 15
    Op-edEliezer Yudkowsky, Pausing AI Developments Isn’t Enough. We Need to Shut it All Down, TIME, 29 Mar 2023.The canonical failure. The strongest available platform, given to the field’s most-identified figure, and the only paragraph that survived into public memory was the one about airstrikes on rogue datacentres. In a general-audience op-ed you do not get to choose which paragraph is the piece — the most extreme sentence is the piece.
  2. 35
    PaperBender, Gebru, McMillan-Major & Mitchell, On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots, FAccT ’21.The other tradition. A student who cannot state this paper’s argument without sneering is not equipped to talk to half the room. You will be asked to state it in the session, and you will be graded on whether its authors would recognise your summary.
  3. 35
    PaperGebru & Torres, The TESCREAL bundle, First Monday (2024).Long. Read the argument closely, skim the genealogy. This is the most sustained available account of why a large, serious constituency reads AI safety as an ideology rather than a field. Disagreeing with it is allowed; not having read it is not.
  4. 20
    PaperBengio, Hinton, Yao, Song et al., Managing extreme AI risks amid rapid progress, Science (2024).The consensus-committee register at its most disciplined. Highlight every hedge, then ask what each hedge is doing — some are honesty, some are the price of a signature. Learning to tell them apart is most of institutional writing.
  5. 15
    ManifestoMarc Andreessen, The Techno-Optimist Manifesto (2023).Read last, once you have sympathy for the safety side, and read it as writing. Its moral inversion — that deceleration “will cost lives… a form of murder” — is better political prose than anything the movement it attacks has produced. Sit with that rather than resolving it.

Core reading 90 min
Live 120 min
Artifact out Published + plan

Ship it, and get in

You will publish, and you will find out that almost nobody is hiring.

The paradox, stated without spin. Across roughly twenty core organisations checked on 2 September 2026 there were fewer than a dozen genuinely comms-shaped open roles worldwide — METR, Apollo, OpenAI, UK AISI, Encode and Constellation at zero. The technical evaluation labs do not employ communicators, and a course implying otherwise is lying to its learners. Yet a February 2026 survey of 59 AI safety leaders ranked advocacy and policy engagement the most under-resourced parts of the field, with technical safety tenth of fourteen, and named talent rather than funding as the binding constraint.

So the on-ramp is not “apply to open roles.” It is make the role exist, or be the obvious hire when one appears — which is how the CAIS, Epoch and MIRI comms functions actually came into being.

Seminar case

Ozone and the Montreal Protocol against the anti-GMO backlash, taught as a pair. Same instruments — vivid naming, a villain industry, precaution over proof. One was right and won in two years; one was wrong and won for thirty. From inside a campaign, is there any way to tell which one you are in — and what evidence would make you stop?

Roleplay

The created-role pitch. Each student pitches a role that does not exist to a skeptical executive director, with their published artifact as the evidence.
